01 · Motivation

When school stress makes takeout the default, “healthy” advice needs to feel like home.

After moving to the U.S. for my MBA, I realized how easy it was to stop cooking. Busy weeks, unfamiliar grocery aisles, and stress can turn ordering unhealthy food into the path of least resistance.

I wanted to build a small kitchen companion for students—especially international students who have just moved countries—that makes cooking feel possible again. The goal is not to replace a person’s cuisine with a generic diet. It is to help them feel at home while making informed, sustainable choices about the food they already love.

“HomeDish helps me return to the dishes that carry a memory, then shows me a gentler way to cook them on a busy week.”

The problem

Context

A new kitchen

Ingredients, portions, and cooking habits change when someone moves across countries.

Pressure

A busy schedule

When cooking feels hard to plan, convenience food wins—even when it does not support the user’s goals.

Mismatch

Generic swaps

Advice that ignores spice, texture, technique, or identity is unlikely to be cooked twice.

Design question

Can a photo-to-plan workflow make culturally familiar cooking healthier without making the cook feel corrected? HomeDish answers with three commitments: recognize uncertainty, keep the user in the loop, and preserve the dish’s signature.

North star: a lighter choice that still belongs at the table.

02 · What is new

HomeDish turns recognition into a user-editable, culturally aware handoff.

Many food-photo experiences end with a label or a single calorie estimate. HomeDish uses the photo as a starting point: the model proposes what might be present, the cook edits the assumptions, Python calculates ranges from bundled USDA data, and a second model step proposes swaps that still resemble the original meal.

My view of the new market angle is the image-recognition component as an action trigger. Compared with the competitor set reviewed, HomeDish is designed to create a real “let’s replace this” moment: the photo does not just tell the user what they ate; it opens an editable path toward a healthier version of the same dish. Recognition becomes the beginning of a decision, not the end of a food log.

The “let’s replace this” moment. See the familiar plate → identify its likely high-impact ingredients → choose a culturally legible replacement → recalculate it → cook it. This is the product’s distinctive handoff from image recognition to action.

03 · Agents, tools, and model

A bounded agent chain surrounds a deterministic nutrition core.

The production path uses Streamlit for the interface and FastAPI for the vision/calculation boundary. Portkey routes the vision and language calls to gpt-6-luna. Pydantic schemas validate every structured handoff; the bundled USDA table and Python tools own the arithmetic.

The user sees a recommendation, not a trace of the code.

The current interface uses a top navigation bar rather than a sidebar. A person can move from Home to Discover, Planner, Pantry, Cook Mode, Grocery, or Recipe Box; Preferences live in the top-bar popover. The calculation internals remain hidden behind a recommendation-style result.

07 · Safety

Make the unsafe path short, visible, and testable.

HomeDish treats image instructions, non-food uploads, malformed model output, allergies, medical claims, and unexpected spend as product risks. The design puts a boundary around the model instead of asking it to be trustworthy by tone alone.

RiskDefense in the current buildEvidence status
Prompt injection in an imageRecognizer instructions treat visible text as data, never as instructions; downstream math uses confirmed fields.Implemented in prompt contract; adversarial screenshot test pending.
Non-food uploadRecognizer returns is_food: false and the pipeline stops before calculation or swaps.Implemented; end-to-end capture pending.
Hallucinated numbers / IDsNutrition values come from deterministic Python; matcher accepts only IDs in its candidate set.Covered by nutrition and matcher tests.
Malformed output / loopsPydantic validation, one repair retry, one critic revision, maximum ten LLM calls.Implemented; expanded malformed-payload matrix pending.
Allergens / medical claimsCritic flags allergy strings and phrases such as “cures diabetes” or “safe for diabetics.”Medical-claim rejection test passes.
OverspendingImage resizing, per-run $0.05 cap, per-run ten-call cap, and clear budget exceptions.Implemented in config.py; priced test pending.

Human control

The user edits the ingredient table, ranges, servings, and recipe choice. The app does not silently turn an uncertain image into a fixed medical recommendation.

Scope boundary

HomeDish is decision support for cooking. It is not a diagnosis, treatment plan, allergy clearance, or guarantee of nutrition accuracy.

Safety language shown to users: nutrition estimates are ranges, not medical advice. If an ingredient or allergen matters, the user must verify the label and recipe independently.

08 · Impact and evaluation

The product promise is testable; broad impact claims would be premature.

HomeDish is intended to reduce the distance between “I recognize this food” and “I can cook a version this week.” The current build demonstrates the workflow and its controls, but it does not yet establish recognition accuracy, nutrition accuracy, weight-loss outcomes, or user adoption.

What success would look like

Users correct the assumptions rather than abandoning the flow; selected swaps preserve a dish’s identity; the resulting grocery list is actionable; and the measured range coverage improves after edits without exceeding the cost cap.

09 · Limits, next steps, and sources

Make uncertainty useful, then learn where the system still fails.

Limits today

  • Photos cannot reliably reveal hidden oil, sugar, stock, cooking loss, or portion size.
  • USDA matches are imperfect for regional preparations and mixed dishes.
  • “Authentic” is plural and contextual; a model score cannot decide cultural legitimacy.
  • User edits improve the estimate but add friction, especially for a tired student.
  • External recipe images and source pages can change or disappear; production needs licensed/local assets.

Next build

  1. Capture permissioned screenshots and a short real end-to-end demo.
  2. Run the 8–10 dish ground-truth evaluation, reporting coverage and error.
